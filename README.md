# puppy crate: How to Size and Choose the Right One, Avoid the Classic Mistakes, and Earn Cashback When You Buy

The crate decision usually lands on you in the same week as everything else — the vet visit, the food transition, the chewed phone charger. And the advice you get is either "just get the biggest one" or "crates are cruel," both of which are wrong.

What actually matters is smaller and more practical: the crate has to fit the dog you'll have in six months, it has to survive whatever your puppy does to it, and the money you spend on it and everything that goes inside it is money you can claw back a little on. This is a walk through all three.

## Start with a tape measure, not a product page

The sizing rule veterinarians and trainers keep repeating is short. A crate should be tall enough for your puppy to stand up without hitting the top, sit upright, turn around, and lie down stretched out. That's it. Brand labels ("medium," "large") vary enough between manufacturers that they're close to useless on their own.

Get two numbers before you shop:

- Floor to the top of the head (standing)
- Base of the tail to the tip of the nose (lying stretched out)

Then add roughly four inches to both, which is the margin most pet-care guides recommend so the pup has room to move without feeling boxed in.

The counterintuitive part: a crate that's *too* big causes problems. Dogs instinctively avoid soiling where they sleep, and that instinct is what makes crates so effective for house training. Give a puppy a crate the size of a studio apartment and it will happily designate one corner as the bathroom. That's not defiance, it's geometry.

## Buy for the adult dog, then shrink the space

This is the workaround that saves you from buying three crates in a year. Purchase for your dog's expected full-grown size and use a divider panel to wall off the extra space while the puppy is small, then move the divider back as it grows. Crates sold through pet-supply stores commonly include dividers; if yours didn't come with one, a sturdy box that the puppy can't chew and swallow works as a temporary blocker.

If you already know the puppy will top out at 15 pounds, you can skip the divider logic entirely and buy a small crate. If you're bringing home a Labrador, plan for the big one from day one.

## The four crate types, and what each one costs you later

Price differences between these are real, but the more important differences are durability and where the crate can legally go.

| Crate type | Best for | Advantages | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Collapsible wire | Medium to large breeds, home use | Folds flat, easy to clean, good ventilation, dog can see out | Dogs can bend or damage bars trying to escape; chewing wires can injure teeth or jaws; not airline approved; not sturdy enough to protect a dog in a crash |
| Plastic (hard-sided) | Den-minded dogs, travel | Enclosed sides feel secure, warmer inside, messes stay contained, airline approved, moderate crash protection | Not collapsible, bulky to store, harder to see your dog inside |
| Soft-sided / fabric | Small and medium pups, travel | Light, portable, packs down for trips | Less stable for a puppy that's still testing escape routes; sharp puppy teeth can chew through fabric |
| Metal box-style | Dogs that outsmart everything | Very sturdy, hard to escape or chew through, bar spacing reduces jaw-injury risk | Heavy, expensive, needs dedicated storage |

Two more categories worth knowing. Crash-tested crates exist, with Sleepypod, Variocage, Gunnar, and RuffLand commonly cited as examples, and they're the only ones built for the specific job of protecting a dog in a car. They're expensive and generally don't collapse. And if a crate isn't working for you at all, alternatives are legitimate: an exercise pen or play yard, or a baby gate across a puppy-proofed room. Gates need to be fastened to a solid surface, since freestanding ones tip easily.

## Travel and crash safety: the spec product pages skip

Wire crates are fine in a living room and wrong in a moving car. If the crate slides or flips, a dog inside a wire crate has less protection than you'd assume, and the same crate isn't accepted by airlines. Plastic kennels are the standard for flying and give reasonable protection in a vehicle; crash-tested crates are the top tier.

That's a second purchase for a lot of owners, and it's why experienced breeders suggest having more than one: one next to your bed, one wherever the family hangs out, and ideally one that stays in the car.

## The first-month shopping list nobody mentions

The crate is the headline purchase, but it's rarely the only one. The standard starter list includes a crate pad or bed, a cover, favourite treats, and a treat-dispenser toy. Add in a few specifics from the care guides:

- Bedding that won't wrinkle up underneath the puppy, ideally running up the sides of the crate for warmth. Veterinary bedding works, and so do washable blankets.
- A lightweight blanket over part of the crate to create a den feel, as long as you keep ventilation open and it doesn't get too hot.
- A long-lasting chew or food-stuffed toy, which is what actually keeps a puppy calm inside the crate rather than merely confined.

That's four or five separate transactions at a pet retailer before the puppy has its adult teeth. Which is exactly where the next section matters.

## How long can a puppy stay in a crate?

This is the number people get wrong most often, and getting it wrong is how crate training turns into a fight.

- **Overnight** is fine and common. Puppies are social animals and are used to sleeping in a pile with their mother and littermates, so the crate should start close to your bed, where they can sense you and where you'll hear the potty signal. Most puppies can get through the night without a break by around four months; younger ones usually can't, and expecting a small-breed puppy to hold it all night isn't fair on the dog or on your carpet.
- **Daytime** is where the caps come in. Veterinary and welfare guidance commonly puts a young puppy at roughly four hours maximum during the day, with the general advice that dogs shouldn't be crated for more than a few hours at a stretch. Breeders' guidance is blunter: never leave a puppy in a crate all day, because it needs bathroom breaks, food, and play.
- **Age-based tables** exist too. One veterinary blog's schedule starts at up to about 60 minutes for an 8 to 10 week old, scaling up as bladder control develops.

A crate is a management tool for the moments you can't supervise. It isn't a full-day solution, and it isn't a substitute for exercise.

## The habits that make crate training fail

Most of this is about association, and the mistakes are consistent:

1. Shutting the door on night one. Veterinarians recommend building a positive association with the door open, with treats and toys placed inside, and not closing it until the puppy chooses to rest in there on its own.
2. Letting the puppy cry it out. Letting a puppy cry builds a negative association with the crate and makes the next attempt harder. Work in short sessions and let the dog out before it escalates.
3. Using the crate as punishment. Repeatedly flagged as the single most damaging habit. The crate should only be associated with calm, rest, and good things.
4. Treating the crate as a cure for separation anxiety. It isn't one. If a puppy is salivating, vocalising persistently, or trying to escape, that's a signal to speak to a vet or a qualified behaviour professional rather than push through.
5. Doing it all alone when you don't have to. Some breeders start crate familiarity before the puppy goes home, which makes your job much easier, and it's worth asking about.

One more thing worth saying plainly: a crate at night isn't mandatory. At least one well-known trainer argues that owners who don't want to crate on night one shouldn't feel forced into it. If the crate is going badly and the dog settles fine next to you, that's a legitimate setup too.

## What to do this week

Order of operations, if you want the crate sorted before the puppy is home:

1. Measure your puppy and add about four inches to the height and length. If you don't have the puppy yet, get the breeder's or shelter's estimate of adult size.
2. Pick the type based on your home and your car, not on price alone. Wire for the living room if you're not flying or driving far; plastic if you are; crash-tested if the dog rides in the car regularly.
3. Buy for the adult size with a divider, or accept buying twice.
4. Build the full list before you check out: crate, divider, pad or bedding, cover, chew toy, treats. One order beats four.
5. [👉 Set up the cashback account first](https://bit.ly/ShopbacK) so the sign-up bonus and the order cashback are working on the same purchase, then start every future trip from the platform rather than typing the retailer's address.
6. Introduce the crate with the door open, treats inside, and no rush. Close the door for seconds before you close it for minutes.
7. Keep the crate next to your bed for the first weeks, set an alarm for the night toilet break, and don't let the puppy cry it out.

The crate is a small purchase with an outsized effect on the first six months. Get the size right, get the type right, and let the cashback be a small refund rather than the reason you bought anything.
